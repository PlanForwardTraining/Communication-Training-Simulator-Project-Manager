# Scenario 05: Resetting a Client Who Is Micromanaging The Crew

> **Status:** Placeholder content. Replace with your real project examples before going live.

## Setup

You are the PM on a primary-suite addition for the **Romanos**. The work is roughly 40% complete. The client — a successful tech executive — has gradually shifted from engaged to **disruptive**. You have decided that today, **Thursday afternoon**, you are going to ask for a 30-minute call to **reset the working relationship**.

This is **your call to make**, not a reactive one. The client doesn't yet know it's happening. You have asked for "a quick conversation about how we work together going forward." They've agreed. They likely think it's a project status check.

## The Pattern You've Observed

Over the last three weeks, increasingly:

- Client is **on site daily**, sometimes twice a day. Two weekends in a row they came by Saturday morning.
- Client gives **direct instructions to trades**, often contradicting the foreman. Yesterday they asked the painter to *"just touch up that one spot"* on a different scope, which derailed the painter's day.
- Client texts you **15-20 times per day** with photos and questions, including outside business hours.
- This morning, the client sent a **9-page email** demanding a daily written status report from each trade.
- Two weeks ago, client overrode the foreman's mortar choice on the fireplace. The foreman complied. The client now wants it changed back. That's a $1,800 redo.

## The Impact On The Project

You are not bringing all of this to the client, but you need to know it walking in:

- **Schedule has slipped 3 days** in the last week, attributable to churn from contradictory instructions. This is recoverable but only if the pattern stops.
- **Crew morale is deteriorating.** The foreman has privately told you that two trades have threatened to walk if "this keeps up."
- **Quality risk** is rising — workers are starting to do what they think the client wants rather than what the spec says, to avoid conflict.

## What You've Already Tried

- Two weeks ago you asked the client to **route questions through you, not the trades**. They agreed in the moment. Held for about three days.
- Last week you set up a **shared photo log** so they could see daily progress without coming to site. They use it but still come to site.
- You've started **batching responses** to their texts to once-per-morning and once-per-evening. They've adapted by sending more.

The softer interventions are not working. This is now a **structural conversation**, not another nudge.

## What The Client Believes

- They see themselves as **engaged and detail-oriented**, not as a problem.
- They believe their on-site presence is **preventing mistakes** ("I caught the mortar issue, didn't I?").
- They are likely **anxious about the investment** — this is the biggest renovation they've done. Their behavior is fear, not arrogance.
- They are **unaware** of the impact on the crew or the schedule. No one has told them.

## What You Want Out Of The Call

A specific, structured agreement on how the project runs from here. Bring it ready to propose:

- **Weekly Tuesday-morning walk-throughs** with you (45 minutes). Client can ask anything.
- **One weekly written status email** from you, every Friday EOD, covering progress + look-ahead.
- **One designated text channel — to you only**, not directly to trades. Reasonable response window: same-day during business hours.
- **Trades take all direction from the foreman.** The client routes any concern through you.
- **A simple change-order rule:** any change costing >$500 or affecting schedule requires a written sign-off, no verbal redirects on site.

You are **not** asking the client to disengage. You are asking them to engage *through a different door*.

## Inside The Client's Head

The client is going to feel **personally criticized** the moment they realize this conversation is about their behavior. Their immediate reactions, in rough order:

1. *Wait — am I being told I'm not allowed in my own house?*
2. *Are you saying I'm the problem? I'm trying to help.*
3. *If I weren't watching, what else would be slipping?* — fear, dressed up as defiance.
4. *Is this conversation actually a setup for them to ask me to back off entirely?*

Underneath all of it: this client is paying a lot of money and is afraid they're not getting what they paid for. Their anxiety expresses itself as control. That is the real thing the PM is addressing — not the texting count.

## How They Will Likely React

- *"I'm just trying to make sure things are done right."*
- *"It's my house and my money. I'll be on site whenever I want."*
- *"Are you telling me I'm not allowed to talk to people working on my property?"*
- *"Other clients don't get this kind of treatment. Why am I being singled out?"*
- *"If I weren't watching, things would be even worse."*
- **Defensive escalation** — *"I'd like to talk to the owner about this."*
- A version where they say very little, agree to everything, and then keep doing what they were doing.

DISC overlays:
- **D / D-I clients** will be sharp and challenging. Match their pace, don't soften, but don't escalate.
- **C / D-C clients** will demand evidence. Be ready to give one or two specifics — *not* a full prosecutorial brief.
- **I clients** will get hurt feelings before logic. Lead with the relationship.
- **S clients** are the trickiest in this scenario — they will agree to everything on the call and not change a thing. The PM has to make the structure concrete and actually confirm understanding.

## What Success Looks Like

- The client agrees to the structure — Tuesday walk-through, Friday email, single text channel, foreman directs trades, written sign-off on changes.
- The client does **not** feel scolded, dismissed, or pushed away. They leave the call feeling more cared-for, not less.
- The crew can do their work. The schedule recovers.
- The client's anxiety is **named and addressed** — the PM articulates *why* they think the client has been showing up so much, with empathy. Doing this well is what makes the structure stick.
- No ultimatums. No bringing up the trades' frustration in a way that makes the client feel ganged up on. No threats about the schedule.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Being passive.** *"It would be great if maybe we could think about…"* — the client will run right over it.
- **Being passive-aggressive.** Listing grievances dressed up as observations.
- **Making the client feel like the bad guy.** They think they're being a good owner. Honor that frame.
- **Bringing up the trades' frustration.** *"The crew is unhappy"* — turns the client into the heel and rallies them defensively.
- **Skipping the structure proposal** and only delivering the "please stop" message. Without an alternative, the client is being asked to do nothing — which feels like abandonment.
- **Caving the moment they push back.** Teaches the client that pressure works. The PM has to hold.
- **Going too hard.** Being harsh with a client this anxious doesn't reset them — it makes them dig in.

The path is **kind, unflinching, structured.** The PM names the anxiety, validates it, offers a better way to channel it, and holds the structure when pushed.

## Coaching Focus

This is the most demanding **DISC Adaptation** scenario in the catalog — every profile reacts very differently to the same conversation. It's also a heavy test of **Confidence & Composure** (the PM has to hold a position warmly across pushback) and **Empathy & Acknowledgment** (the conversation lives or dies on whether the client feels respected, not just managed). **Solution Orientation** is judged on whether the structure proposal lands as a complete package the client can actually agree to. **Active Listening** matters because the PM has to hear what's underneath the client's defense — usually fear about the investment — and address that, not just the surface complaints.
