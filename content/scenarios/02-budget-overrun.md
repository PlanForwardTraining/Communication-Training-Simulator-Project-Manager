# Scenario 02: Communicating a Budget Overrun

> **Status:** Placeholder content. Replace with your real project examples before going live.

## Setup

You are the project manager on a whole-home renovation with a contracted budget of **$485,000**. The project is currently 60% complete. You need to deliver news that the projected final cost will be **$542,000** — a **$57,000** overrun, roughly **12%** above contract.

## What's Happened

Three drivers of the overrun, in order of dollar impact:

1. **Hidden conditions ($28,000):** When the wall between the kitchen and dining room was opened, the trades found unpermitted electrical work from a 1990s renovation. Code now requires the entire panel to be upgraded and the affected circuits to be brought up to current code. This is a documented hidden-condition allowance per the contract.
2. **Client-driven scope additions ($19,000):** Over the last three months, the client has approved **eleven** small change orders — a wine fridge, upgraded primary-bath tile, mudroom built-ins, exterior paint color change after the first coat, etc. Each was a few thousand dollars; cumulatively they're material.
3. **Material cost increases ($10,000):** Lumber and trim package came in over the original estimate due to a price hike between contract and order date. Documented in the original contract as subject to market.

## What the Client Knows

The client has signed every change order for #2 above, so they should remember those. They've never been told the running total of those changes — each was approved in isolation. They received written notification of the hidden-condition allowance use ($28K) but only as a line item on a weekly status email, which they may or may not have absorbed.

## What the PM Must Communicate

- The total projected overrun: $57,000 / 12% over contract
- Clear breakdown of the three drivers with documented backup ready to share
- Which portions are contractually covered vs. require a new change order
- What the remaining 40% of the project includes and what (if any) further risks remain
- A specific request: schedule a 30-minute follow-up to walk through the documents in person

## Desired Outcomes

- Client understands exactly where every dollar is going
- Client does not feel that costs were hidden from them or sprung at the last minute
- Client recognizes that **$19K of the overrun is from their own approved changes** — without the PM saying that defensively
- Client agrees to a written acknowledgment of the new projected total
- The PM secures a follow-up meeting to review documentation rather than trying to resolve everything on this first call

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- Leading with the dollar figure before the context — clients shut down emotionally
- Defensive blame-shifting onto the client for the change orders (true but counterproductive)
- Vague language like "things have come up" or "we've had some surprises"
- Promising to "absorb" any of the overrun on the spot
- Treating the conversation like a transaction — clients need empathy first, math second
- Skipping the request for a follow-up meeting and trying to settle it all in one call

## Realistic Client Pushback

- "We had a budget. That's why it's called a budget."
- "I never agreed to a $57K overrun."
- "Why is the company eating any of this? You should have caught the electrical issue earlier."
- "Show me every signature." (And the PM should be ready to)
- "What's the absolute ceiling? I need a number that won't move."
- Quiet anger — which often shows up as overly clipped, polite responses

A composed PM acknowledges the financial weight, presents the breakdown, and proposes the documentation review as the next step rather than trying to win the argument.
