# Scenario 04: Addressing a Scope Change the Client Did Not Expect

> **Status:** Placeholder content. Replace with your real project examples before going live.

## Setup

You are the PM on a **kitchen remodel** for the **Patels**. The architectural showpiece of the design is the removal of the load-bearing wall between the kitchen and family room — opening up a 12-foot sightline from the kitchen island to the fireplace. The clients have stated more than once that this sightline is the single feature they're most excited about.

The structural engineer reviewed framing photos taken yesterday and determined the **existing beam pocket cannot support the proposed opening**. The issue was discovered before the wall came down — meaning the project is still recoverable without rework, but you need a decision quickly.

You are calling the client today, within 24 hours of the engineer's finding. The wall is scheduled to come down Monday.

## The Engineer's Finding, In Plain Language

The original architectural plans assumed a beam pocket of one size. When the trades opened the ceiling to verify, the actual structure is framed differently than the plans implied — the existing pocket can't carry the load of a 12-foot opening as drawn. The engineer's letter describes this as a **legitimate hidden condition**, not a design error.

## The Two Options

The engineer has put forward two viable paths:

### Option A — Larger Steel Beam
- Adds **$14,000** to the budget
- Adds **5 days** to the schedule
- **Preserves the full 12-foot opening** the client designed around
- Requires new column supports at each end of the beam, slightly intruding into the room corners

### Option B — Smaller Opening
- **No cost change**
- **No schedule change**
- Opening reduces from 12 feet to **8 feet**
- The kitchen-to-fireplace sightline is **lost** — the client's headline feature is gone
- Visually, the room reads as two connected rooms rather than one

The engineer is comfortable with either option. Both are fully code-compliant. There is no "right" answer — it's a values trade-off the client owns.

## The Decision Window

The wall comes down Monday. To keep the schedule intact, the decision needs to be made within **48 hours** so the right beam (or smaller header) can be ordered. After 48 hours, the schedule starts slipping at roughly one day per day of delay.

## What The Company Is Doing

Already authorized to offer the client today:
- The engineering re-review fee (**$1,200**) is being absorbed by the company as a goodwill gesture for the surprise.
- A written packet (engineer's letter, both option estimates with full breakdowns, photos) will be sent within 24 hours of the call.

## What The Client Knows Going In

- They love the design and have specifically referenced the sightline multiple times.
- They have already paid for the original engineering and demo.
- They are not aware of the beam pocket issue. This is the first they will hear of it.
- They tend to make decisions together, not unilaterally — both spouses will likely be on the call or one will need to relay to the other.

## Inside The Client's Head

When the issue is presented, the client will mentally land in one of three places. The PM has to read which one quickly:

1. **Suspicion of an upsell.** *"Wait — the company is suddenly telling me to spend $14K more, and they say it's not their fault?"* This is the most dangerous landing place because it puts every word the PM says next under a credibility microscope.
2. **Decision paralysis.** *"We can't decide this in 48 hours. We need to think about it. Can we get a second opinion?"* Real, valid, and a schedule killer.
3. **Trust the team.** *"OK, what would you do?"* The PM has to be careful with this one — recommending a path is fine, but the decision must remain the client's.

## How They Will Likely React

Expect some combination of:
- *"How did the original engineer get this wrong?"*
- *"Why am I paying for this?"* (You can immediately note the $1,200 absorption.)
- *"Can we get a second opinion?"* — a fair question. Don't dismiss it.
- *"What would you do?"* — answer honestly, then return ownership.
- *"We can't decide in 48 hours."* — name the schedule cost without applying pressure.
- **Spouse disagreement** in real time — one prefers Option A (preserve design), the other prefers Option B (no cost). This is realistic. The PM should not take sides.

DISC overlays:
- **D** clients want crisp options and a recommendation. They'll decide on the call. *"Which one do you recommend?"*
- **C / D-C clients** will want every number documented and may push back on the cost breakdown. Have it ready.
- **S** clients will want to talk to their spouse, look at it overnight, and come back tomorrow. The PM can grant 24 hours but should be honest about what 72+ costs.
- **I clients** will respond emotionally first ("but the sightline!"), then come back to logic. Give them space.

## What Success Looks Like

- The client understands that the issue is **real and structural**, not a sales pitch for an upgrade.
- The two options are presented with **honest, equal-weight tradeoffs** — not steered toward Option A.
- The $1,200 engineering absorption is mentioned as a quiet gesture, **not** as evidence the company is doing the client a favor.
- If asked for a recommendation, the PM gives one **honestly** — based on what they think this client values — and immediately reaffirms that the choice is the client's.
- The 48-hour decision window is named clearly. Not as pressure — as a fact about the schedule. *"After Friday, every day of delay is a day on the back end."*
- The client receives the written packet within 24 hours.
- The client makes a decision within 48 hours and the schedule holds.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Making the call sound like an upsell pitch.** Subtle enthusiasm for Option A reads as motivated.
- **Burying the schedule impact of Option A.** *"Just a few extra days"* — be specific (5 days).
- **Telling the client which option to pick.** Even if asked, frame the recommendation as *"if I were in your position with what you've told me matters most"* — and then return ownership.
- **Avoiding the 48-hour window.** It feels like pressure, so PMs soften it. The result is a 5-day decision and a schedule slip the client didn't know they were causing.
- **Treating the $1,200 absorption as a bargaining chip.** It's a quiet line in the conversation, mentioned once.
- **Letting spouse disagreement become a debate the PM mediates.** The PM's job is to give both spouses the facts — not to tip the scale.

## Coaching Focus

This scenario primarily tests **Clarity & Honesty** (translating engineering findings into plain language without spinning), **Solution Orientation** (the quality of how options are framed), and **Confidence & Composure** when the client questions the company's competence. **DISC Adaptation** is critical for managing the decision-making style — pushing a C client to decide on the call is as bad as letting an S client take a week. **Active Listening** matters most when spouse disagreement surfaces; the PM should hear both before responding to either.
