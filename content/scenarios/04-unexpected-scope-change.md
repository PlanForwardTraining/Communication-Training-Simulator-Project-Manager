# Scenario 04: Addressing a Scope Change the Client Did Not Expect

> **Status:** Placeholder content. Replace with your real project examples before going live.

## Setup

You are the PM on a kitchen remodel. You need to inform the client that the **original load-bearing wall removal cannot proceed as designed**. The structural engineer reviewed framing photos taken yesterday and determined the existing beam pocket is undersized for the proposed opening. The remedy requires either:

- **Option A:** A larger steel beam with new column supports — adds **$14,000** and **5 days** to the schedule, but preserves the open-concept design.
- **Option B:** A smaller opening (8 feet instead of 12 feet) — no cost change, no schedule change, but the kitchen-to-living-room sightline the client fell in love with is gone.

## What's Happened

- The original architectural plans assumed a beam pocket size that turns out to be incorrect; the existing structure was framed differently than the plans implied
- This is a **legitimate hidden condition**, documented and supported by the engineer's letter
- The discovery was made yesterday afternoon; the PM is calling within 24 hours

## What the Client Knows

- They selected this design specifically because of the open sightline from the kitchen island to the family room fireplace
- They have already paid for the original engineering and demo
- They are not aware of the issue yet

## What the PM Must Communicate

- The structural finding, in plain language
- The two viable options with honest tradeoffs
- The PM's recommendation (if asked) — but the client makes the final call
- The decision needs to happen within **48 hours** to keep the schedule from slipping
- The company will absorb the engineering re-review fee ($1,200) as a goodwill gesture

## Desired Outcomes

- Client understands the problem is real, not a sales pitch for an upgrade
- Client feels they are in control of the decision, not pressured
- Client receives written documentation (engineer's letter, both option estimates) within 24 hours of the call
- The client makes a decision within 48 hours
- The client's confidence in the team's competence is preserved — discovering the issue **before** cutting the wall is presented as the system working correctly

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- Making the call sound like an upsell pitch for Option A
- Burying the schedule impact ("just a few extra days")
- Telling the client what to choose instead of laying out tradeoffs
- Avoiding the 48-hour decision window because it feels like pressure
- Letting the client take a week to decide and then watching the schedule slip
- Treating the goodwill engineering fee as a negotiating chip rather than a quiet gesture

## Realistic Client Pushback

- "How did the original engineer get this wrong?"
- "Why am I paying for this?"
- "Can we get a second opinion?"
- "What would you do?"
- "We can't just decide in 48 hours, we need to think about it."
- Spouse disagreement during the call — one wants Option A, the other Option B

The PM's job is to be a **trusted advisor, not a salesperson**. Lay out the facts cleanly, answer questions honestly, give the client space to decide, and protect the schedule by being clear about timing without being pushy.
