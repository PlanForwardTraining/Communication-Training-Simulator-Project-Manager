# Scenario 01: Delivering a Schedule Delay

> **Status:** Placeholder content. Replace with your real project examples before going live.

## Setup

You are the project manager on a kitchen and primary-bath remodel for a family that has been living in a rental unit since demo started. The original completion date promised at contract signing was **June 15**. You now need to call the client to deliver news that the project will not be done until **August 1** — a six-week slip.

## What's Happened

Two compounding issues:
1. The custom cabinetry order was placed correctly, but the manufacturer's plant had a fire in their finishing department three weeks ago. Replacement cabinets are being run, but the new delivery date is **July 10** — four weeks later than original.
2. While framing the new bathroom, the trades discovered the existing subfloor had significant water damage from a previously unknown slow leak. Remediation and structural repair added another **2 weeks** to the schedule.

Neither issue was caused by the company. Both are real, documented, and covered by the contract's force-majeure and hidden-conditions clauses.

## What the Client Knows

The client has **not** been told about either delay yet. They know there was "a small issue with the cabinets" from a casual mention at last week's site visit, but they believe the project is still on track for June 15. They are currently paying $4,200/month in temporary housing and have already extended their lease once.

## What the PM Must Communicate

- The new completion date: **August 1**
- The two underlying causes (cabinet fire, subfloor damage)
- That the company has already taken steps to mitigate (expedited shipping on cabinets at company cost, weekend crews on framing)
- What the client should expect over the next two weeks
- Acknowledgment of the financial and emotional impact on the family

## Desired Outcomes

- Client hears the full truth without feeling blindsided or misled
- Client understands the issues were outside the company's control but the company is taking ownership of the recovery
- Client leaves the call with a clear next step (next site visit date, written timeline update by end of week)
- Trust is preserved or strengthened, not damaged
- No verbal commitments are made that the company cannot keep (e.g., "I promise we'll be done before August" — never)

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- Burying the lede ("everything is going great, BUT…")
- Over-apologizing in a way that signals incompetence rather than ownership
- Blaming the cabinet manufacturer or the trades by name
- Offering financial concessions on the spot without authorization
- Making the call about the PM ("I feel terrible about this") instead of the client
- Using construction jargon the client doesn't know

## Realistic Client Pushback

Expect some combination of:

- "Why am I just hearing about this now?"
- "Are you going to cover our extra rent?"
- "What guarantees do I have that August 1 is the real date this time?"
- "Can you put another crew on it?"
- "I'm going to need to talk to my attorney about the contract."
- Silence, then a flat "OK" — which is often the hardest reaction to read

The PM's job is to absorb the reaction without becoming defensive, and to leave the client feeling heard before pivoting to the recovery plan.
