# Scenario 01: Delivering a Schedule Delay

> **Status:** Placeholder content. Replace with your real project examples before going live.

## Setup

You are the project manager on a kitchen and primary-bath remodel for the **Hartleys**, a family of four living in a rental unit since demo started. The original substantial-completion date promised at contract signing was **June 15**. You are now calling to deliver news that the realistic completion date is **August 1** — a six-week slip.

You are calling at **10:30 AM on a Wednesday**, which the client previously indicated is a good time. You should expect the call to last 15-25 minutes.

## What's Happened

Two compounding causes, both legitimate, both documented:

1. **Custom cabinetry — manufacturer fire (4-week impact).** The cabinetry order was placed correctly and on time. Three weeks ago, the manufacturer's finishing department had a fire. Replacement cabinets are being run on an expedited line. New delivery date is **July 10**, four weeks later than the original.
2. **Subfloor water damage (2-week impact).** While framing the new bath, the trades pulled up flooring and discovered significant water damage from a previously unknown slow leak. Remediation and structural repair of the joists added two weeks.

Neither cause was avoidable by the company. The cabinet fire is force-majeure under the contract. The water damage falls under the hidden-conditions clause.

## What the Client Knows Going In

- They believe the project is still on track for **June 15**.
- They heard a casual mention from the foreman last week about "a small issue with the cabinets" but have not been told about a delay.
- They are paying **$4,200/month** in temporary rent and have already extended their lease once.
- They have a daughter starting middle school in September and have been counting on being moved in well before then.

## What You Already Have In Hand

The company has already taken steps you can offer the client today, without further authorization:

- The cabinet manufacturer is paying for **expedited shipping** at no cost to the client.
- The framing crew is running **Saturday shifts at company expense** to absorb a portion of the subfloor delay.
- A written **revised timeline document** is ready to send by end of day, including weekly milestones from now to August 1.
- The contracts team has the force-majeure and hidden-conditions clauses pulled and bookmarked if the client wants to see them.

You **cannot** offer rent reimbursement, lease-break support, or any financial concession on this call without owner approval.

## Inside the Client's Head

The client is going to feel **blindsided**. Six weeks is not a small slip. Their primary fears, in rough order:
1. *We're going to be in this rental forever and bleeding money.*
2. *What else are they not telling us?*
3. *Is this date even real, or will it slip again?*
4. *Do we have any recourse here?*

The financial impact is real ($4,200 × 6 = $25,200 of additional rent, before considering the daughter's school timing). They will likely raise it.

## How They Will Likely React

Expect at least three of these:
- *"Why am I just hearing about this now?"* — the most common opener
- *"Are you going to cover our extra rent?"*
- *"What guarantees do I have that August 1 is real?"*
- *"Can you put another crew on it?"*
- *"I'm going to need to talk to my attorney about the contract."*
- A long silence, then a flat *"OK."* — often the hardest reaction to read

DISC-specific overlays:
- A **D** client will demand the bottom line first, then options. Don't bury the date.
- A **S** or **S/C** client will go quiet, may seem accepting on the call, but the relationship damage compounds in silence — the PM has to explicitly ask "what is going through your head right now?"
- A **C** client will want every cause documented and may ask for the engineer's letter and the manufacturer's correspondence. Have it ready.
- A **I** or **D/I** client may emote heavily up front and recover quickly if the PM stays warm and concrete.

## What Success Looks Like

- The client hears the new date — **August 1** — clearly within the first 90 seconds. No burying.
- The client understands both root causes without the PM blaming the manufacturer or trades by name.
- The client leaves the call with a concrete next step: a written timeline document by EOD and a site visit on the books.
- No commitments are made that the company can't keep — especially nothing about being done "before August."
- Trust is preserved or strengthened. The relationship does not require repair after this call.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Burying the lede.** Anything that sounds like *"everything is going great, but…"* tells the client you're afraid of them.
- **Over-apologizing.** Repeated *"I'm so sorry"* signals incompetence rather than ownership.
- **Naming names.** *"It's the cabinet supplier's fault"* makes the company look small. The PM owns the recovery regardless of root cause.
- **Promising relief on the spot.** No rent reimbursement, no contract concessions, no "we'll make this right financially" without owner approval.
- **Making the call about the PM.** *"I feel terrible about this"* recenters the conversation on the wrong person.

## Coaching Focus

This scenario is primarily a test of **Clarity & Honesty** (delivering hard news cleanly), **Ownership & Accountability** (taking the recovery without deflecting), and **Confidence & Composure** under sustained financial-impact pushback. **Empathy & Acknowledgment** matters most in the first 90 seconds; **Solution Orientation** matters most in the last 5 minutes (the next-step commitment).
